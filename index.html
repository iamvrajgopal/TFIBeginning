The school and its neighbourhood

Varsha Nagar: learning in the middle of Mumbai

Varsha Nagar Municipal School sits at the intersection of two very different Mumbais. On one side, high-rise buildings climb into the skyline; on the other, dense lanes of informal settlements stretch across the hillside.

Most of my students come from families of migrant workers—auto drivers, daily wage labourers, small food stall operators, carpenters, and shop owners. Many live in compact 1RK homes shared by 4 to 10 family members. Space, time, and quiet for study are luxuries, not guarantees.

Families balancing long work hours with the hope of better education for their children.
Children often responsible for siblings, chores, and errands before and after school.
Limited access to private tuitions or digital learning support.
Strong community networks and a deep sense of resilience and humour.

Academic starting point

When maths feels like a burden, not a language

Years of disrupted schooling during Covid meant that many students missed foundational concepts in mathematics. In our baseline, 29% of students scored below 35%, and only 5% secured a first grade. For most children, maths and science were subjects to memorise—not ideas to understand.

Formulas were learnt by heart, steps were copied from the board, and success was measured by how much could be remembered the night before an exam. Without concrete experiences or visualisation, the subjects felt dry, abstract, and intimidating.

When I joined, the grade had gone without a consistent class teacher for more than a month. Class culture had slipped—students were used to irregular periods, frequent free time, and very little structure. Many children arrived without breakfast, bringing low energy and low motivation into already demanding lessons.

Early days in the classroom: rebuilding routines, revisiting basics, and slowly making maths visible again.

What stood out

Behind the low scores: vibrant, energetic children

Even as assessments painted a bleak academic picture, the children themselves were far from dull. They were lively, playful, and eager to talk and move. Games on the ground, jokes in the corridor, and curiosity about the world around them were always present.

The challenge was to channel that energy into learning—to design activities, experiments, and simulations that allowed them to see, touch, and experience maths and science. If concepts could be made visible and connected to real life, understanding and confidence could begin to grow.
